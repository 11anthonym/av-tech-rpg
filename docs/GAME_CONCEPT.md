# AV Tech RPG: Game Concept

## Elevator Pitch

Play as an AV integration technician completing commercial, education, and
government projects. Survey rooms, interpret work orders, install equipment,
route systems, solve failures, and survive the gap between the drawing set and
the actual site conditions.

The tone should be grounded and playful. The player gets the satisfaction of
understanding a complicated room, finding the one thing that is wrong, and
making the system work five minutes before turnover.

## Genre

*AV Tech RPG* is an audio visual technician role-playing game. Jobs act as quests, tools and
certifications unlock new approaches, and reputation opens more demanding
contracts. The role-playing progression should support the troubleshooting
gameplay rather than turn the project into a spreadsheet.

## Core Player Fantasy

The player becomes the person who can walk into a room full of equipment and
make sense of it.

This is a game about practical competence:

- Reading a room before touching anything.
- Choosing a workable installation approach under constraints.
- Building clean signal paths.
- Diagnosing failures from symptoms.
- Balancing craftsmanship, schedule, budget, and client expectations.

## Candidate Core Loop

1. Accept a job with a scope, drawings, budget, and deadline.
2. Survey the site and notice constraints or contradictions.
3. Plan equipment placement and cable routes.
4. Install devices and connect the system.
5. Configure and test the room.
6. Troubleshoot failures, including mistakes and unexpected field conditions.
7. Commission the system and receive a score.
8. Spend earnings and reputation on tools, certifications, and larger jobs.

## First Playable Slice

Start with one classroom retrofit. The room needs a flat panel display, a wall
plate input, a small rack, speakers, and a control point.

The player should be able to:

- Inspect a simplified work order.
- Place required equipment.
- Connect a visible signal chain.
- Route cables while respecting distance and pathway constraints.
- Use a tester or symptom clues to find a fault.
- Complete a commissioning checklist.

Possible first fault: the display reports no signal because an extender endpoint
is connected incorrectly. This keeps the diagnosis visual and understandable.

## Job Families

### Education

- Classroom display retrofits.
- Lecture halls with multiple sources and microphones.
- Campus-wide summer refreshes with repeated rooms and a tight deadline.
- Training rooms where instructors need a simple control workflow.

### Commercial

- Conference rooms and divisible spaces.
- Executive boardrooms where finish quality matters.
- Digital signage deployments.
- Video walls and all-hands meeting spaces.

### Government

- Council chambers and public meeting rooms.
- Secure briefing rooms with access and documentation constraints.
- Courtrooms with recording and evidence presentation systems.
- Operations centers with redundancy requirements.

## Systems Worth Exploring

- **Signal flow:** Connect sources, transport, processing, amplification, and
  endpoints in a legible way.
- **Troubleshooting:** Use symptoms, test equipment, and process of elimination.
- **Site conditions:** Discover blocked pathways, missing power, wrong dimensions,
  late equipment, and drawing conflicts.
- **Craftsmanship:** Reward clean routing, labeling, rack layout, and documentation.
- **Client handoff:** Make the final user experience matter, not only whether the
  underlying system technically functions.
- **Career progression:** Unlock better tools, certifications, helpers, and more
  complex job types.

## Tone And Presentation Questions

These are intentionally open:

- Is the game a cozy first-person or top-down job simulator?
- Is equipment represented realistically or with readable fictional stand-ins?
- Do projects happen room-by-room, or does the player also manage a crew and
  schedule across a building?
- How much humor should come from recognizable field situations?
- What should the player's in-game integration company be called?

## Prototype Guardrails

- Prefer readable systems over exhaustive realism.
- Teach one AV concept at a time.
- Let the player understand why a fix worked.
- Keep the first room small enough to replay quickly.
- Treat domain accuracy as flavor and structure, not as a barrier to entry.

## Next Design Decisions

1. Choose a camera and interaction style.
2. Decide whether the first prototype is 2D or 3D.
3. Pick the engine based on that prototype.
4. Define the classroom signal chain and the exact failure state.
5. Sketch the commissioning score categories.
